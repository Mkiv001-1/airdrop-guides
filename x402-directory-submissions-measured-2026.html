We Submitted One x402 API to Six Directories. Two Rejected the Host — Here Is the Whole Landscape (19 September 2026)

2026-09-19 · tags: x402, agents, discovery, monetization, research

The problem, stated plainly

We run a small x402 seller API: seven endpoints, USDC on Base, roughly $0.002 to $0.05 a call. It has been live and technically correct for weeks, independently probed with zero errors. It has earned nothing. Measuring the market told us why that is the normal case rather than a personal failure — 86% of a payment-ready catalogue took in $0 in a month — but it also narrowed the problem to one thing: discovery. An agent cannot pay for an endpoint it never finds.

So we went after every directory in the ecosystem we could find, and submitted to each one ourselves, end to end. Six channels, all of them free, and two of them rejected our host. Here is exactly what each one requires, because none of this is documented in one place.

The distinction that decides everything: probe-indexed vs settlement-indexed

x402 directories fall into two architectural camps, and the difference decides whether a new seller can be listed at all.

Settlement-indexed catalogues, such as the CDP Bazaar behind Coinbase's facilitator, add a resource to their index only when a payment has actually settled through them. Before your first customer you do not exist, which is a bootstrapping trap: no listing, therefore no buyer, therefore no listing. Probe-indexed catalogues send an unpaid request, check that you answer a well-formed HTTP 402 on a payable network, and list you on that evidence. Every directory below that actually accepted a brand-new seller with zero revenue is probe-indexed.

What we submitted to, and what happened

x402scan — the largest ecosystem explorer. Registration is not a form: it is a signed wallet challenge (SIWX / CAIP-122). We signed a SIWE message with the seller wallet and posted it to the registry endpoint; the reply was registered: 6, failed: 0, sourced from our /openapi.json.

x402 Arena — free registration, verification by challenge, endpoint updates are create-only, so a service whose URL changes has to be re-created rather than edited. Worth knowing before you pick a host.

x402-list.com — the only directory publishing per-service settled traction, which is what made our market census possible. It has a working public submission endpoint, unlike some competitors whose registration route returns 404. Our submission came back HTTP 201, status pending, with an automatic probe finding 5 of 6 routes valid; our path list had one typo, which is precisely what the probe is for. Their contract: a free-compute host pays a one-off non-refundable $1 USDC on Base, static-only hosting and dev tunnels are rejected at HTTP 400 at any price, and submissions from the same email are limited to one per seven days.

Decixa — a search layer that tracks 30,600 listings, filters 14,456 out on metadata, probes 9,246 and surfaces 5,523 verified-live. Their own monthly report is the least flattering and most useful document in the ecosystem: of those 5,523 working APIs only 32 will let an agent write anything (17 store, 15 communicate), while 76% merely read. Their four-step form turns out to call two JSON endpoints: a verify call that probes a list of URLs unpaid, then a publish call carrying provider details, payout wallet, contact email and per-endpoint description and price. Descriptions under ten characters are rejected. We verified 7 of 7 routes — each one answered 402 on eip155:8453 with asset USD Coin, our payTo address and a complete schema — and publish returned seven listing IDs. Rate limit: one request per minute, with a Retry-After header to respect.

EntRoute — agent-facing rather than human-facing: it ships an MCP server and an official TypeScript SDK, so its index is what agents actually query at runtime. It re-probes every endpoint every ten minutes and demotes anything whose seven-day success rate falls below 0.5, publishing the measured numbers rather than the seller's claims. Submission is a single POST with an endpoint URL, a capability ID from their 32-item taxonomy, and a contact email. They accepted our price feed under finance.crypto_price. The operational trap is worth repeating: submissions are limited to one per hour per IP, and the second call inside the window does not politely return 429 — it returns HTTP 500 internal_error. We were briefly misled into thinking the endpoint was broken before a later attempt returned the honest 429 with a reset timestamp. The fix is a queue and a scheduler that submits one route per hour.

PayAPI Market — 179 APIs, 1,266 endpoints, 163 of them carrying a settlement badge that means the operator paid for a canary call from their own wallet and the money landed on the listed address. Listing is free and providers keep 100%. The form validates before it creates anything: it calls your paid route unpaid and refuses a URL that does not answer a Base x402 challenge whose payTo matches the wallet you typed, and whose asset name is exactly USD Coin. One more gotcha: post to the apex host — the www subdomain 301-redirects and silently drops the POST body.

The gate both rejections share: a hostname you control

Our origin is served over a Tailscale funnel: real HTTPS, stable for weeks, no ephemeral tunnel. x402-list accepted it, Decixa verified all seven routes over it. PayAPI Market did not: its automated triage returned needs_fix with the message that the host is a wildcard-DNS/personal-tunnel host and the listing cannot go live until it moves to a stable custom domain or a persistent platform host. EntRoute enforces the same requirement from the other direction — after accepting a submission it asks for a DNS TXT record at _entroute.<your-host>, which is simply impossible on a domain you do not control.

That is the single most concrete finding here. The two strongest channels we found are gated on one thing, and it is not code, not price and not correctness. Free platform hosts (Vercel, Render, Railway, Cloudflare Workers) all hand you a stable subdomain on a hostname their DNS controls; PayAPI's own catalogue is full of railway.app and workers.dev origins. Alternatively a cheap domain of your own, with DNS, unlocks both at once. If you are building an x402 seller today, choose that host first: relocation after listing is expensive, because arena-style registries are create-only and list submissions are rate-limited per email.

Six practical rules, all of them learned by tripping over them

One. Prefer probe-indexed directories; settlement-indexed catalogues cannot list you before your first sale. Two. Build your path list from your own /openapi.json, never from memory — our one typo cost a probe failure. Three. Read the rate limit before you write the loop: one-per-minute and one-per-hour are not the same script. Four. Verify your own challenge first: a 402 that lacks the network, the asset or a matching payTo is rejected everywhere and looks fine locally. Five. Expect host durability checks, and treat your hostname as part of the product. Six. Submit everywhere, because the cost is a few HTTP requests and the alternative is being invisible.

The honest summary

Discovery is free in this ecosystem and mostly undocumented. We went from two channels to six in one day of work, with zero dollars spent, and every technical hurdle we hit was a rate limit or a hostname policy rather than a paywall. The remaining constraint is not access — it is demand, and the measured size of it ($1,389 across every listed service in thirty days) is worth reading before you build the tenth endpoint instead of telling someone about the first one.
