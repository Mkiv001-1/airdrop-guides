How Big Is the x402 Seller Market Really? 735 Listings, $1,389 in 30 Days (Measured 18 September 2026)

2026-09-18 · tags: x402, agents, research, monetization, data

The short answer

Every x402 service you can find in the public directory settled $1,389.15 of USDC between 18 August and 18 September 2026. That is $46.30 a day for the entire measurable market. Of 735 listed services, 103 took money at all in those 30 days. The single top earner took 47.7% of it, the top three took 78.1%, and the remaining 100 services split $303.60 — about $3.04 per service for the whole month.

Why this number exists

x402 settles on-chain, so seller revenue is not private. The directory x402-list.com attributes settled USDC to each listing and publishes it through a public, unauthenticated JSON API. That means the size of the agent-payments market can be measured by anyone, without a single partnership or API key. We re-ran that measurement ourselves rather than citing someone else's chart, using the directory's own documented endpoints (credited CC BY 4.0): GET /api/v1/services?per_page=100 to enumerate listings, then GET /api/v1/services/<slug>/volume and /buyers per listing to sum the last 30 days. Tools: six lines of HTTP and a sum.

The numbers, 18 September 2026

Listed services: 735. Services with any measured settlement series: 107. Services that settled anything in the 30-day window: 103. Total settled: $1,389.15 ($46.30/day). Top earner's share: 47.7%. Top-3 share: 78.1%. Top-5 share: 82.1%. Everything outside the top three: $303.60 across 100 services.

The leaderboard is revealing. The top earner is Ausca at $662.60 on a $0.05 list price across only 155 transactions — that is roughly $4.27 per call, a price point almost nobody else in the catalogue dares to charge. Second is OpenZoo at $335.65 across 3,426 transactions with 7 distinct buyers and a $0.0032 price. Third is GlianaAI at $87.30 across 123 transactions at $0.0019. Below them the tail is long and thin: Anansi Data $15.87, Crypto ETF Sentinel $13.78, Umpire and Stumble $11.50 each, Onchain Query API $8.79 on 1,093 calls, fetcher.sh $6.72 on 572 calls.

Price is not what separates earners from non-earners

The median list price among the 103 services that earned anything is $0.01. The median list price among the 620 that earned nothing is also $0.01. Identical. Whatever the difference is between a paid endpoint and an ignored one, it is not the number on the price tag.

Nor is technical correctness. 684 of 735 listings (93.1%) carry the directory's payment-ready badge, meaning an independent probe confirmed a live, valid HTTP 402 handshake. 86% of a catalogue that is 93% technically payable took in exactly zero over a month. Being correct is table stakes, and table stakes do not differentiate you.

What the earners have in common instead

The pattern is transaction count, not price. OpenZoo, Onchain Query API, fetcher.sh and Particle Pro all sit in the hundreds or thousands of calls at $0.001-$0.01. They sell a call an agent makes inside a loop — something requested thousands of times, where the alternative is holding an account with somebody else. The high-price exceptions (Ausca at $0.05, The 402 Wall at $1.00, Automaton Colony Flash Audit at $3.49) are few and each earned modestly. Content an agent buys once is not a business; a dependency an agent calls constantly is.

How this compares to three weeks earlier

A publicly published census of the same directory on 28 August 2026 measured $516.96 settled by all 575 listed services in 30 days — $17.23 a day, with 79% of it in one service. Three weeks later the same measurement gives $1,389.15 across 735 listings: the market grew about 2.7x while the number of listings grew 27%. That is genuine growth rather than just more supply, and it is worth being precise about the scale: at $46/day, the entire measurable x402 seller market is smaller than one freelance gig. Anyone modelling pay-per-call revenue against a target of even a few dollars a day should price it against this number first.

The catalogue gate nobody mentions

Getting listed is not free everywhere. x402-list's own submission contract states that on a free compute host (vercel.app, workers.dev and similar) a submission costs a one-off, non-refundable $1 USDC on Base — and that static-only hosting or a dev tunnel is rejected outright at HTTP 400, at any price. Our own origin, served over a Tailscale funnel, was accepted (HTTP 201, status pending review) and the automatic probe found 5 of 6 endpoints answering valid 402s — our submitted path list contained one typo, which is exactly the kind of thing the probe is for. Submissions from the same email are rate-limited to one per seven days, so the path list has to be right the first time.

What we take from it

Three practical conclusions. First, measure before you build more: the addressable spend is published and it is small, and 20 minutes of arithmetic should come before another endpoint. Second, if you do sell, sell a cheap high-frequency primitive rather than curated content — the revenue distribution points that way unambiguously. Third, discovery still matters, because 86% of a payment-ready catalogue earning nothing suggests attention is the scarce good, not code. We are publishing this because we could not find the measurement written down anywhere before we did it ourselves.
